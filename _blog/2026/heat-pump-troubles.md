Few things provide as much ragebait to me as critical systems which - without explanation - refuse to work. Take a heatpump for example. Critical infrastructure around the home. These are expected to provide warmth when it's cold, and to cool when it's hot. When such system fails it isn't so much experienced as merely just an alert on some \[hardware/software] dashboard but instead as bodily discomfort.

Troubleshooting these issues should be as simple as identifying the cause of the perceived failure, and either resolving it or (temporarily) overriding the systems behaviour. As I state this I nearly hear the scream of a thousand voices rise up; "how can we expect the end-user to be in control of a system as complex as a heat pump". Oh the arrogance. How does some end-user expect to be in control of such a complex system?

One problem here is the enormous gap between user controls and expert controls. While the user is generally expected just to control the temperature knob, the expert is expected to test the individual components of the installation. There is - unfortunately - no in between. If the system does not obey my wishes for a certain temperature setpoint it does not leave me any recourse. The system does what the system does, and the end-user may go screw themselves. If you truly want people to offer their firstborns then design systems which do what they do without explaining themselves.

The more complex a system is the more irrational it may behave. All of these problems could have been somewhat negated having proper documentation on what goes on inside the algorithm, but with the software industry being what it is the likes of anyone being able to explain anyways is close to zero. It is why systems should be simple, and if simple is no longer a viable possibility, it should at least be able to explain what the heck is going on.

A lot comes together in this episode with the heat pump. A slight understanding of the complexity of modern systems. The arrogance technical folks generally have towards users. The perceived stupidity of end-users and an implicit assumption they are unable to learn about systems as a need arises. The state of the software industry in general. The lack of technical quality. The lack of proper documentation. The lack of imagination about what maintainable systems look like. The locking away of systems behind cloud services. The power asymmetry perpetuated through systems design.

Do better. It is possible. There are some people showing it does not have to be like this. Take [BBQKees](https://bbqkees-electronics.nl/) as an example.
